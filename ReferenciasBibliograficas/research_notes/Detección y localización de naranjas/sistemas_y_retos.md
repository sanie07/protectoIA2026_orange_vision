# Integrated vision-guided harvesting and yield-estimation systems for oranges and citrus: field validation and open challenges

Research date: 2026-11-04. Evidence tags used on every finding:
- **[FT]** full text read (local PDF or retrieved open-access text)
- **[ABS]** abstract or publisher/index metadata only; methods not checked
- **[SNIP]** search-result snippet only; treat as a lead and verify before citing
- **[VENDOR]** company-reported claim
- **[TRADE]** trade press

Crop labels: **CITRUS** is citrus or orange. **NON-CITRUS** is any other fruit and is included only as a nearby reference.

## Which robotic citrus/orange harvesters have been built and tested, and what were the field results?

### Takeaway
Published fully integrated citrus field results cluster at about 82-87% picking success and 5.6-10.9 s per fruit, but these come almost entirely from abstracts, with test sizes usually unreported in what could be read, and I found no independent multi-season commercial benchmark. Several headline "citrus" results are lab-only, on artificial trees, or semi-manual, and the commercial systems (Energid, Nanovel) have no peer-reviewed harvest figures.

### Inferences
- Citrus field success (82-87%) is not clearly better than the best documented apple field results (about 80%). The apple figures rest on far larger, better-described trials, so the citrus numbers should not be read as evidence that citrus is further ahead.
- Comparing the table's numbers directly is unsafe: the metric (per-attempt, per-fruit, "arm success"), whether unreachable or heavily occluded fruit were excluded, and fruit counts differ or are unknown.
- The only paired citrus figures found differ by about 5 points (87.15% vs 82.4%), and their conditions are not clearly defined, so this is not a true lab-field comparison. No paired lab and field protocol on the same citrus robot was found, so the size of the gap in citrus is currently unmeasured rather than known to be small.
- The most useful result for a thesis framing is the persistent split between detection scores (often above 90%) and end-to-end picking success (about 80-87%), which shows that detection accuracy alone does not determine system performance.

### Gaps
- Fruit counts, trial duration, occlusion filtering and localization error for Yin et al. (2023) and Xiao et al. (2024) could not be read; only abstracts were accessible (Wiley and MDPI returned 403). These are priority full-text reads.
- I found no 2024-2026 fully autonomous vision-guided citrus picking robot from IVIA, UPV or another Spanish group. This reflects the searches run, not proof that none exists; RiuNet and ReDivia were not searched directly.
- Whether Bao et al. (2024) was a field or lab experiment was not confirmed.
- A search summary attributed "127 of 150 fruit, 84%, 6.0 s, 7-DOF" to a citrus harvester. These figures match Silwal et al.'s **apple** robot as described by Tang et al., so I did not use them for citrus. A "three-finger clamp, 95.23%, 4.65 s" snippet had no identifiable source and is also excluded.
- No peer-reviewed Nanovel or Energid harvest data were found.

## How do perception pipelines integrate with the arm: visual servoing vs. look-then-move, eye-in-hand vs. fixed camera, multi-arm coordination, picking order and task planning?

### Takeaway
Citrus work splits between early closed-loop eye-in-hand servoing (Florida) and today's mostly RGB-D look-then-move pipelines, with hybrid global-plus-local vision repeatedly proposed. Quantified citrus evidence comparing these architectures in a real orchard is nearly absent; the closest direct comparison uses an artificial tree, and picking-order planning for citrus is supported by a single dual-robot paper with no field-validated multi-arm result.

### Cited Findings

**Visual servoing and camera configuration (CITRUS)**
- Taxonomy from a citrus-specific source: open-loop dead reckoning (Ceres 1998; d'Esnon 1987) is vulnerable to fruit motion; Levi et al. (1988) is susceptible to backlash, bearing wear and slippage; Harrell's closed-loop camera-in-hand offers high-frequency feedback that passively compensates motion but is sensitive to outdoor lighting. **[FT]** — Mehta S.S., MacKunis W., Burks T.F. (2014). "Nonlinear Robust Visual Servo Control for Robotic Citrus Harvesting." 19th IFAC World Congress, pp. 8110ff. [PDF](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/ifac2014/media/files/2729.pdf)
- That paper proposes a **cooperative fixed camera plus camera-in-hand**: the fixed camera gives the global canopy view, and the in-hand camera servos to the target. It adds a robust term for bounded fruit motion from wind or robot-tree contact, with Lyapunov uniformly ultimately bounded regulation, avoiding the need for high-frequency image feedback. Results shown are analytical and simulation-style. **[FT, abstract and intro; result sections scanned only]** — same source
- Mehta S.S., Burks T.F. (2014). "Vision-based control of robotic manipulator for citrus harvesting." *Computers and Electronics in Agriculture* 102:146-158. Described as a closed-loop estimation and control system combining a fixed camera and a camera in hand. **[FT of secondary source; no abstract available]** — [Font et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3390/s140711557); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compag.2014.01.003)
- MacKunis W., Mehta S.S., Burks T.F. (2016). "Robust Visual Servo Control in the Presence of Fruit Motion for Robotic Citrus Harvesting." *Computers and Electronics in Agriculture* 123. Simulated fruit displacement of 35-210 mm; experiment on a 7-DOF redundant manipulator with an **artificial citrus fruit moving 120 mm**. No published tracking error or orchard data on the record page. **[ABS]** — [ERAU record](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/robust-visual-servo-control-in-the-presence-of-fruit-motion-for-r/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compag.2016.03.007)
- University of Florida progress reports describe visual servo improvements from a statistical range estimate and homography as "promising as long as the target is visible", and note the prototype arm lacked the range, speed and cost efficiency needed commercially. **[SNIP]** — [UF citrus mechanical harvesting](https://citrusmh.ifas.ufl.edu/index.asp?s=8&p=2)

**Direct configuration comparison (CITRUS-shaped target, artificial)**
- Beldek C., Dunn A., Cunningham J., Sariyildiz E., Phung S.L., Alici G. (2025). "Multi-vision-based Picking Point Localisation of Target Fruit for Harvesting Robots." arXiv:2502.12406. Artificial orange on an artificial tree, with motion-capture ground truth. Mean Euclidean distance: eye-in-hand single camera 24.02 mm, analytical two-camera 14.25 mm, AdaBoost regression 4.40 mm. Picking: 77.7%, 81.4%, 88.8%, with 27 random positions and three attempts per position. The surface-to-centre problem is the key cause: the camera sees a surface point, not the fruit centre; a 35 mm z offset was used for eye-in-hand. **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12406)
- **Caveat:** the 35 mm-radius sphere assumption made the data nearly linear (linear regression MED 4.15 mm in the same table), so low errors may not transfer to variable real fruit. **[FT]** — same source

**Look-then-move with RGB-D (typical recent pattern)**
- Gursoy et al. compute the fruit centre from depth at the bounding-box centre plus a fruit-radius offset (ellipsoid assumption), then derive the peduncle point from the vertical semi-axis. Assumption fails when fruit does not hang downward, which the authors flag. **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08729)
- Ye et al. (2025): YOLOv5s ROI plus depth point cloud and oriented box, converted by hand-eye transform to the end-effector goal. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture15222330)
- Xiao et al. (2024): deliberately uses a compliant gripper so mechanical tolerance can make up for perception inaccuracy. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture14081227)
- Zhu et al. (2026, **NON-CITRUS: apple**): with a fixed ToF-based look-then-move system, recommends mounting a camera on or near the end-effector for servoing in the final approach, because small visible fruit fragments shift the estimated pick point away from fruit centre. **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14089)
- Font et al. (2014, **NON-CITRUS**): stereo in the gripper, rough approach by inverse kinematics to about 250 mm (4.2 s mean), then closed-loop centring on fruit centroid (7.9 s mean), stopped by a fruit-area threshold because the view no longer contains the whole fruit at short range. A clear hybrid look-then-move and servo template, and it exposes the close-range limit of stereo. **[FT]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/s140711557)
- Depth sensing requirements: Neupane C., Koirala A., Wang Z., Walsh K.B. (2021). *Agronomy* 11(9):1780 (**NON-CITRUS-specific**, orchard fruit localization). Set a bias-corrected RMSE specification of 20 mm at 2 m in sunlight; only ToF cameras met it; Azure Kinect at 1.5 m in sunlight had 6 mm RMSE and 17 mm bias. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy11091780)
- Tang et al. (2020) favour passive binocular stereo outdoors and describe ToF as sensitive to strong light, which **conflicts** with Neupane et al.'s later measured sunlight results; sensor generation probably explains part of it. **[FT]** — [Tang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2020.00510); [Neupane et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy11091780)

**Fruit selection, order and multi-arm coordination**
- Citrus dual-arm control: Gursoy et al. define cutting as the absolute task (fixed frame) and collecting as the relative task, solved by Hierarchical Quadratic Programming with joint limits and collision constraints via velocity dampers. The trunk is modelled as an infinite-height cylinder from HSV and depth masks. Selection is greedy, nearest fruit first, and a planner is listed as future work. Branches are not modelled, and interaction with them displaced fruit. **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08729)
- Citrus picking order: Bao et al. (2024) scan first, then solve order as TSP with a genetic algorithm in about 0.12 s. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1049/tje2.12419)
- **NON-CITRUS (apple) field evidence for coordination:** Zhu et al. (2025) 28% harvest-time reduction against single arm **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atech.2025.101343). Zhu et al. (2026) use a temporal-logic specification for shared vacuum and failure recovery **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14089)
- **NON-CITRUS (apple), snippets only:** Li et al. (IROS 2023) multi-agent RL for a four-arm planner; a figure of 79.31% and 5.8 s appears in the text but it is unclear whether it is their result or a cited one **[SNIP]** — [arXiv:2303.00460](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00460). Dual-arm GA sequence: 76.97%, 7.29 s, 5.56% damage **[SNIP]** — [PMC12430792](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12430792/)
- Learned end-to-end alternative (CITRUS-like lab): Hu H. et al. (2026), "HarvestPoint-ACT." arXiv:2608.18446. ### Inferences
- Beldek et al. suggest a useful hypothesis: much of "localization error" with a wrist camera is geometric (surface versus centre) and could be corrected cheaply by a fruit model, before investing in new sensors. Testing this on real, variable oranges is an open and modest experiment.
- The field-validated reasons for adding closed-loop correction (wind, contact-induced motion, partial views) are well argued, but I found no citrus orchard trial that quantifies servoing against look-then-move on the same robot.
- Task planning matters less per unit of compute than one might expect, since sequence planning times are small (0.12 s). Its benefit should be judged on total harvest time and on avoiding fruit disturbance, not on solver speed.

### Gaps
- No orchard A/B test of servoing vs. look-then-move for citrus was found.
- No field multi-arm citrus results with picking-order optimization were found. Nanovel has six arms but no published data, and arXiv:2505.10028 on multi-Cartesian-arm scheduling was seen only in a snippet with crop and test setting unconfirmed.
- Localization error measured **inside an integrated citrus system in the field** was not available. Only artificial-tree values (Beldek) and pose angle error (Xiao, 7.9°) were found.
- The camera setup in Yin et al. (2023) and Xiao et al. (2024) could not be confirmed from abstracts.

## Which fruit-counting or yield-estimation pipelines use tracking or 3D localization to avoid double counting in citrus?

### Takeaway
DeepSORT remains the common citrus baseline, but both a citrus custom tracker (OrangeSort) and a 3D-relocalization pipeline (Santos et al.) show that occlusion handling, more than detector quality alone, controls counting error. Direct citrus evidence for ByteTrack is limited to one 2026 UAV paper, and only one citrus pipeline was found combining SfM with a validated tree-level yield model.

### Cited Findings

**Citrus: 2D tracking**
- Zhang W., Wang J., Liu Y., Chen K., Li H., Duan Y., Wu W., Shi Y., Guo W. (2022). "Deep-learning-based in-field citrus fruit detection and tracking." *Horticulture Research* 9:uhac003. **Citrus: 3D relocalization and yield**
- Santos T.T., de Souza K.X.S., Camargo Neto J., Koenigkan L.V., Moreira A.S., Ternes S. (2023). "A pipeline for multiple orange detection and tracking with 3-D fruit relocalization and neural-net based yield regression in commercial citrus orchards." arXiv:2312.16724 (Embrapa with Fundecitrus; preprint; journal version not checked). The most complete pipeline found:
  - **Tracking:** CNN detection, Hungarian IoU assignment, and COLMAP SfM for camera poses. Tracks with at least 5 boxes are triangulated with RANSAC and DLT, oranges are modelled as spheres, and lost tracks are reprojected to re-match detections.
  - **Sensitivity to simulated detection rate:** at 100% detection HOTA 0.935, MOTA 0.973, counting error 2.34%; at 80% HOTA 0.712, error 3.01%; at 60% HOTA 0.466, error 14.44%; at 40% HOTA 0.198, error 55.93%.

  - **Yield:** a feed-forward regressor uses counts from both tree sides, variety, height and age. R² = 0.85 once at least 30% of fruit is accurately detected, tracked and counted.
  - **Data:** MOrangeT, 12 annotated tracking sequences with 3D-assisted annotation; OranDet, 57,812 tiles and 222,201 boxes. Fundecitrus ground truth for 1,543 trees; regression count stated inconsistently as 1,139 and 1,197.

YOLOv8 plus ByteTrack plus SfM, with 3D fruit clustering for the final count. Demonstrated near a small orange tree with 12 fruits, so this is a proof of concept rather than a yield validation. **[FT scan, partial]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18293)

### Inferences
- Santos et al. give the clearest quantitative argument for 3D: below about 60-80% detection the pipeline degrades sharply, and without relocalization count error is very large. This implies a threshold detector requirement for reliable counting, under their simulation assumptions.
- Citrus MAE, MAPE, MOTA and "counting accuracy" values cannot be ranked across papers: scenes, ground truth (manual video counts vs. harvest) and error units differ. Zhang's MAE 0.081 appears to be a relative measure and is not comparable with Si's MAE 9.98 in fruit.
- Tree-level "visible count" and true yield are different quantities. Only Santos et al. among citrus papers read here explicitly bridge them with a validated regression at scale.
- Tracker choice is less decisive than occlusion handling: custom region counters, 3D relocalization and tuned association all outperformed unmodified trackers.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head of unmodified DeepSORT vs ByteTrack on a common citrus dataset was found. Weng et al. is the closest and tests its own modified ByteTrack.
- No LiDAR-based citrus yield mapping and no absolute 3D fruit-position error for citrus were found in the searches.
- Comparison of counts with actual harvested weight or count is only verified in Santos et al.; whether the others do this was not determined from abstracts.
- I did not read Santos et al.'s final regression error tables or check for a peer-reviewed version.

## What do recent reviews (2020 to 2026) identify as main remaining challenges and research directions?

### Takeaway
Reviews consistently name occlusion and illumination robustness, 3D positioning accuracy, integration of perception with planning and manipulation, generalization across varieties, annotated-data dependence, and speed and reliability. They also agree, directly or implicitly, that there is no standard benchmark, and many still rely on success figures that trace back to Bac et al. (2014).

### Cited Findings
- Tang Y., Chen M., Wang C., Luo L., Li J., Lian G., Zou X. (2020). "Recognition and Localization Methods for Vision-Based Fruit Picking Robots: A Review." *Frontiers in Plant Science* 11:510. Local PDF. **[FT]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2020.00510)
  - Named challenges: recognition under illumination change and occlusion; target tracking under dynamic interference; 3D reconstruction; fault tolerance of vision.
  - Stereo-specific: dynamic scenes change fruit shape between views and enlarge 3D error; stereo matching is computationally heavy; illumination and occlusion reduce 3D accuracy.
  - Directions: small-sample deep learning; visual-mechanical error compensation (random and transmitted errors "easily overlooked"); AI with active fault tolerance; high-accuracy dynamic tracking "remains an unsolved issue".
  - Benchmarks: "no unified and clear indicators" of commercial performance. Example user targets from its survey: litchi at most 15 s per bunch with about 70% picking; tomato 5-8 s and 90%.
- Zhou H., Wang X., Au W., Kang H., Chen C. (2022). "Intelligent robots for fruit harvesting: recent developments and future challenges." *Precision Agriculture* 23(5):1856. Widespread orchard use not yet seen; analyses reasons for inadequate performance and maps challenges to environmental factors and user requirements. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11119-022-09913-3)
- Droukas L., Doulgeri Z., Tsakiridis N.L., et al. (2023). "A Survey of Robotic Harvesting Systems and Enabling Technologies." *Journal of Intelligent & Robotic Systems*. Oranges are treated only as "early interest", with 1990s citations, in its crop table, which shows how thinly citrus features in general surveys. Also records Abundant Robotics' shutdown during COVID-19 for lack of market traction (**NON-CITRUS**, apple). **[FT, passages]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10846-022-01793-z); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10457)
- Kaleem A., Hussain S., Aqib M., Cheema M.J.M., Saleem S.R., Farooq U. (2023). "Development Challenges of Fruit-Harvesting Robotic Arms: A Critical Review." *AgriEngineering* 5(4):2216. Covers 2000-2022; states that no commercial robotic arm yet exists for selective harvest of valuable fruit and vegetables; end-effector design and harvest efficiency are the main interests. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/agriengineering5040136)
- Zhang J., Kang N., Qu Q., Zhou L., Zhang H. (2024). "Automatic fruit picking technology: a comprehensive review of research advances." *Artificial Intelligence Review* 57(3). Efficiency and damage still need improvement. Specific challenges not read. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-023-10674-2)
- Lin T., Sun F., Li X., Guo X., Ying J., Wu H., Li H. (2026). "A Review of Key Technologies and Recent Advances in Intelligent Fruit-Picking Robots." *Horticulturae* 12(2):158. Challenges: large annotated data dependence; sensitivity to illumination and foliage occlusion; limited generalization across varieties; difficulty integrating perception, planning and manipulation into reliable field systems. Trends: lightweight multimodal networks, deformable-object manipulation, embodied intelligence, system-level optimization. **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/horticulturae12020158)
- Failure-mode evidence in place of opinion (**NON-CITRUS: apple**): Zhu et al. (2026) group failures as perception (heavy leaf occlusion), end-effector vacuum failure, and environmental interference (leaves, branches moving fruit); perception failures occurred when visible point clouds were too sparse. **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14089)
- Citrus limits stated by primary papers: arm range and joint errors (Xiao et al. 2024 **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture14081227)); severe occlusion and adhesion at 63.8% recognition (**[FT passage]** — [PMC5982123](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5982123/)); strong sunlight lowers mAP50 to 83.5% against 96.1-98.2% in other light (**[ABS]** — [Si et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.13925/j.cnki.gsxb.20250487)).
- Economics remain a named barrier: Aloisio et al. describe economics as "the key practical problem" **[ABS, VENDOR perspective]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/tepra.2012.6215651)

### Inferences
- Benchmarks: reviews deplore the lack of metrics, but no shared citrus end-to-end benchmark exists. Datasets like MOrangeT, Campaneta and the Fresno set each address one layer. A combined benchmark from detection through 3D localization to harvest outcome would be a clear contribution.
- Generalization evidence in citrus is thin: the visible datasets are from specific regions and varieties, and I found no cross-country citrus evaluation.
- "66% success" is often presented as current but originates in a 2014 survey; recent citrus field values are materially higher, though with weaker and less transparent test designs. It should be cited as a historical baseline, dated clearly.

### Gaps
- A snippet states that field performance is "approximately 5-12 percentage points lower" than simulation in comparable studies. I could not identify the source review with certainty, so it is not cited as a finding; check [Horticulturae 12(2):158](https://www.mdpi.com/2311-7524/12/2/158), which returned 403 on retrieval.
- Full texts of Zhou 2022, Zhang 2024 and Lin 2026 were not accessed; their detailed citrus tables, if any, remain unread.
- No citrus-specific review from 2020-2026 focused on integrated systems was found.

## Are there emerging directions for perception such as foundation models, active perception/next-best-view, synthetic data, or domain adaptation, as applied to citrus or nearby fruit?

### Takeaway
Foundation models have reached field-tested apple harvesters, and active vision has reached lab benchmarks, but I found no citrus study using SAM, DINO or Grounding DINO, and no citrus next-best-view study on a real tree. Domain adaptation is the one area where oranges appear directly, chiefly as source data, so foundation-model perception for citrus looks like a real open niche, subject to the search limits noted below.

### Cited Findings

**Foundation models (NON-CITRUS unless stated)**
- Zhu et al. (2026, apple, field): Grounding-DINO prompted with "apple", then EfficientViT-SAM and depth clustering. mAP@0.5 / mAP@[.5:.95]: pretrained 0.750 / 0.458; after MetaFruit fine-tuning 0.868 / 0.586; after internal-dataset fine-tuning 0.950 / 0.666. Fails when fruit is almost entirely hidden. **Shows that pretrained foundation models still gained substantially from crop-specific fine-tuning.** **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14089)
- Zhu et al. (2025, apple, field): foundation-model detector, pixel segmentation and clustering-based depth estimate "improves performance in outdoor conditions". **[ABS]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atech.2025.101343)
- Grape (vineyard): SAM plus GroundingDINO for zero-shot annotation of RGB-D grape bunches. **[SNIP]** — [ScienceDirect S0168169924010020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168169924010020)
- Cherry and apple tree structure: SAM pseudo-labels in a CVPR 2025 workshop dataset (tree structures, not fruit). **[SNIP]** — [PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~sinisa/research/publications/cvpr25.pdf)

**Active perception and next-best-view (NON-CITRUS)**
- La Greca M.C., Usuelli M., Matteucci M. (2024). "Enhancing Agricultural Environment Perception via Active Vision and Zero-Shot Learning." arXiv:2409.12602. ROS 2 NBV pipeline with a semantic 3D occupancy map; tested in simulation (tomato, grape) and on a lab apple espalier with dense vegetation. Lang SAM (Grounding DINO + SAM) inference about 13.8-18.9 s; YOLO-World + EfficientViT SAM about 2.7-4.1 s, **on CPU**. Outperforms predefined planning qualitatively; numeric gains were in figures I did not transcribe. Segmentation accuracy varied a lot by prompt and model (40-100%). **[FT, partial]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12602)
- Others seen only as snippets: NBV-SC, shape completion for sweet pepper in a commercial glasshouse — [arXiv:2209.15376](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15376); apple disease semantic NBV — [arXiv:2603.23112](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23112); hidden tomato clusters framed as an acquisition problem — [arXiv:2609.18738 via Pith](https://pith.science/paper/2609.18738). **[SNIP]**

**Learning policies under occlusion (CITRUS-like, lab)**
- Hu H. et al. (2026), HarvestPoint-ACT: artificial oranges on an artificial citrus tree. Success falls from 88.5% (least occluded) to 76% (most), and pick time rises from about 12-13 s to 23-29 s. Failures include leaf dragging and fruit falling on contact. Preprint, artificial fruit that needs no peduncle cut, so transfer to real oranges is unproven. **[FT, partial]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18446)

**Synthetic data and domain adaptation (citrus appears directly)**
- EasyDAM (2021): CycleGAN-style transfer from a **labelled orange source** to other species, with pseudo-labelling and self-learning. **[SNIP]** — [PMC8167097](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8167097/)
- EasyDAM_V3: synthetic orchard images on real backgrounds with adaptive pseudo-label thresholds; citrus among target domains. *Plant Phenomics*. **[SNIP]** — [SPJ](https://spj.science.org/doi/10.34133/plantphenomics.0067)
- DomAda-FruitDet: orange as source domain, transferring to apple, tomato, pitaya and mango. *Plant Phenomics*. **[SNIP]** — [SPJ](https://spj.science.org/doi/10.34133/plantphenomics.0135)
- Gursoy et al. (2023) describe COCO-pretrained YOLOv5 retrained on 300 AgriPlant orange images (70:30 split) as "domain adaptation and transfer learning"; this is plain fine-tuning, not unsupervised adaptation. **[FT]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08729)
- Multispectral generalization resource for citrus: Campaneta-Orange-Fruit explicitly lists model generalization as a use. **[ABS]** — [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17866343)

### Inferences
- Grounding DINO's 0.750 to 0.950 progression in apple suggests foundation models are useful as a bootstrapping and labelling tool, not a no-training replacement. Testing this for oranges against a tuned YOLO on Campaneta or MOrangeT would be low-cost and novel.
- Latencies of 13.8-18.9 s on CPU show why embedded deployment remains a practical concern, though GPU and lighter variants (2.7-4.1 s here on CPU) can change this.
- Citrus has a feature that could favour NBV: dense evergreen canopy with strong inner-fruit occlusion. This is a hypothesis from its occlusion figures (63.8%; 76%) rather than a demonstrated benefit.

### Gaps
- The absence of a citrus SAM, DINO or Grounding DINO paper is based on two targeted searches plus index queries, not a systematic review; a focused search on Scopus, Web of Science and Chinese-language sources (CNKI) is still needed before claiming "first".
- EasyDAM-family results were not read beyond snippets, so no accuracy figures are reported; authors and exact citations need to be taken from the source pages.
- No citrus sim-to-real study with quantified transfer performance was found.
- DINOv2 or DINOv3 feature backbones for citrus were not specifically found; dedicated queries were limited.
